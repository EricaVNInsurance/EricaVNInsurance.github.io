What extra benefits might a plan offer?

Understand benefits you may see advertised, from dental coverage to Part B givebacks and help with groceries.

People and situations in the examples are fictional. Dollar amounts are made up to explain the ideas, not a plan quote.

Dental, vision, and hearing

Some Medicare Advantage plans offer help with dental services, eye exams, glasses, or hearing services. The exact services, providers, and amounts covered depend on the plan.

Check exactly what the plan pays for. A benefit name alone does not tell you how much help you will get.

  • Which services are included?
  • Which dentists, eye doctors, or other providers can I use?
  • What will I have to pay?
  • Is there a limit on what the plan pays?
  • Do I have to meet extra requirements?
Example: James needs dental work

James needs dental work and sees that a plan advertises a dental benefit. Before counting on it, he checks whether his exact treatment and dentist are covered and how much of the bill would be his.

Example: James checks the dental limit

Imagine James’s proposed dental work costs $1,600, and his plan pays up to $1,000 for certain covered dental services. He might expect to owe only $600, but the limit alone does not tell him that.

James first checks whether the treatment and dentist are covered, his required share of the cost, and how much of the benefit he has already used. These amounts are made up, not a plan quote.

Part B giveback: a smaller monthly premium

Some Medicare Advantage plans help pay part or all of your Part B premium. This is also called a Part B premium reduction. It is not offered by every plan or in every area.

If you pay your own Part B premium, the reduction can mean less money deducted from your Social Security payment or a smaller Medicare premium bill. It is a premium reduction, not a separate cash payment or spending card. If a program already pays your Part B premium, ask how the benefit would apply to you.

Example: Elena notices a giveback benefit

Elena pays her own Part B premium through a deduction from her Social Security payment. She sees a Medicare Advantage plan offering a Part B giveback.

Before switching, Elena checks how much the plan would reduce her premium, whether her doctors and medicines are covered, and what she would pay for visits and prescriptions. The giveback is one part of her comparison.

Help with healthy groceries

Some plans offer help buying eligible food. What you can buy, where you can shop, the spending limit, and who can use the benefit vary by plan.

Some food benefits are offered through Special Supplemental Benefits for the Chronically Ill (SSBCI). These are for members who meet additional health and care-needs requirements. Having a diagnosis or Medicaid does not, by itself, guarantee this benefit. The plan reviews the member’s health information and confirms whether the particular benefit is available to them.

Example: Maria hears about a grocery benefit

Maria has diabetes and sees a plan advertising help with healthy groceries. She wonders whether that benefit could help her.

Before choosing the plan, she asks what the benefit covers and whether it would be available to her. The plan reviews her health information and care needs to determine that.

Seeing a benefit advertised does not mean every member receives it. The details depend on the plan and the person’s situation.

Over-the-counter items

An over-the-counter (OTC) benefit may help pay for certain health items you can buy without a prescription. Check the plan’s eligible-item list, participating stores or ordering options, and spending deadline. An OTC benefit does not automatically include groceries.

Example: Luis checks before shopping

Luis wants to use his OTC benefit for health supplies. Before buying bandages, he checks that the exact product is eligible and that the store accepts his benefit.

He also checks whether unused money carries over or expires. A product being sold without a prescription does not automatically mean his plan pays for it.

Rides and meal delivery

Transportation

Some plans offer rides for eligible appointments. Check which destinations are covered, how many trips are included, and how far ahead you need to arrange a ride. This benefit is different from emergency ambulance coverage.

Example: Rosa needs a ride to an appointment

Rosa has a doctor’s appointment and needs transportation. She contacts her plan before booking a ride to check whether the trip is covered and how to arrange it.

She does not assume the benefit covers every destination or an unlimited number of rides.

Meal delivery

Some plans offer prepared meals in certain situations, such as for a limited time after a qualifying hospital stay. Check when meals are available, whether approval is needed, and how many are included. Prepared-meal delivery and a grocery allowance are different benefits.

Example: Daniel returns home from the hospital

Daniel’s plan offers a meal-delivery benefit after certain hospital stays. Before he goes home, he asks whether his stay qualifies and how to arrange delivery.

If the benefit is available to him, he checks how many meals he can receive and when the deliveries will end.

Benefit cards and spending limits

A plan may use a card to pay for certain covered items or services. The card is a way to use a benefit; it does not tell you everything you qualify for. Check eligible purchases, participating businesses, separate spending limits, and expiration dates.

Example: Maria checks what her card can buy

Maria’s plan uses a card for eligible OTC purchases. She also sees food benefits mentioned in the plan information, but that does not mean her card is automatically approved for groceries.

She asks the plan which benefits are available to her and how to use each one before shopping.

Compare the whole plan

Check your regular doctors, medicines, and medical costs too. A benefit offered by one plan or in one county may not be available with another. Use the plan’s current benefit summary and coverage documents.
