What is Medicaid?

Understand who it helps, what it may cover, and how to find your state’s program.

Health coverage run by your state.

Medicaid helps pay for health care for people who meet their state’s rules. It is funded by the federal government and states, and each state runs its own program. It can cover children, pregnant people, adults with limited income, older adults, and people with disabilities.

You may know it by another name, such as Medi-Cal in California, MaineCare in Maine, or the Oregon Health Plan. A different name does not necessarily mean a different kind of insurance.

Medicare

Federal health insurance mainly for people age 65 or older, and some younger people with qualifying disabilities or conditions. Income alone does not decide whether you qualify.

Medicaid

State-run health coverage under federal rules. Income, age, disability, household circumstances, and other requirements can affect eligibility. Some pathways also count resources, such as money in certain accounts.

Who may qualify?

There is no single income limit that applies to everyone in every state. The state looks at the coverage group you are applying under. Rules for a child or a working adult can differ from rules for someone age 65 or older or someone who needs long-term care.

  • Income and household: Which income counts and who belongs in your household depend on the program.
  • Age, pregnancy, or disability: These can affect the pathway used to review your application.
  • Resources: Many income-based pathways do not use an asset test. Some age-, disability-, and long-term-care pathways do.
  • Residence and immigration rules: You must meet applicable state residency and citizenship or immigration requirements. Coverage options vary.

Some states cover additional adults through Medicaid expansion. Other states have different coverage pathways. Do not rule yourself out based on a friend’s experience or an old income chart. Read the federal eligibility overview and ask your state to review your situation.

What is a spend-down?

In some states and eligibility groups, medical expenses can help a person qualify when their income is above the usual limit. This is sometimes called a medically needy program or spend-down. It is not the same as giving away savings or property. Ask the state which expenses count and which rules apply before making financial changes.

What can Medicaid help cover?

Medicaid includes required benefits and benefits states can choose to offer. Depending on your coverage, services may include doctor visits, hospital care, lab tests, preventive care, behavioral health care, prescriptions, and transportation to covered medical appointments. Coverage rules and service limits still apply.

Adult dental, vision, therapy services, and some home-based supports can vary by state and coverage group. Children under 21 have special screening and treatment protections through EPSDT, a benefit intended to identify and treat health needs early. Benefits for adults are not always the same.

Check your state’s benefit information and member handbook before assuming a service is covered. See required and optional Medicaid benefits.

Is it always free?

Not always. Some programs have premiums or small copayments, while certain people and services are exempt from charges. The amount depends on your program and applicable rules. Learn about Medicaid costs.

How do I use Medicaid?

Your state may pay providers directly through fee-for-service Medicaid, or arrange care through a managed-care health plan. In managed care, a health plan handles covered services under a contract with the state. Some benefits may be handled separately.

  • Ask whether your doctor accepts your exact Medicaid coverage or health plan and is accepting patients.
  • Check how prescriptions, specialist referrals, and prior approval work.
  • Keep both your state card and health-plan card, if you receive both.
  • Use the member-services number on your card for coverage and provider questions.

A Medicaid health plan is not automatically a Medicare Advantage plan, even if the same insurance company offers both. Learn how Medicaid managed care works.

Can I have Medicare and Medicaid together?

Yes, if you meet both programs’ requirements. For Medicare-covered services, Medicare generally pays before Medicaid. The amount of help depends on the assistance you qualify for. Medicaid may also cover some services Medicare does not.

Full Medicaid and help with Medicare bills are different.

Full-benefit Medicaid provides a package of Medicaid services. A Medicare Savings Program helps with certain Medicare costs. You can qualify for some savings programs without having full Medicaid coverage.

  • QMB: Helps with Medicare premiums and Medicare-covered deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments. Medicare providers cannot bill QMB members for those Medicare-covered cost-sharing amounts.
  • SLMB and QI: Help pay the Part B premium. These programs alone do not mean all medical bills are paid.
  • QDWI: Helps with Part A premiums for certain people with disabilities who return to work.

Compare Medicare Savings Programs and current limits. Extra Help is separate assistance with Medicare prescription costs; it is not proof of full Medicaid. Read our Extra Help guide.

A D-SNP is a Medicare Advantage plan for people who meet its Medicare and Medicaid requirements. Having a Medicaid card does not automatically qualify you for every D-SNP or advertised extra benefit. Your assistance category, location, and the plan’s rules matter. Learn what to check when you have both programs.

What do QMB+, SLMB+, and the other categories mean?

The plus sign means full Medicaid benefits are included alongside QMB or SLMB assistance. The Medicare & Medicaid guide explains all seven main categories, what each helps pay, and how to check which one you have.

How do I apply?

You can apply for Medicaid throughout the year. Start with your state’s official program. The state decides eligibility and sends an approval, denial, or request for more information. Some applications can also start through HealthCare.gov.

The state may ask for proof of income, identity, residence, and other information relevant to your application. People applying under age, disability, or long-term-care rules may need additional records. Send sensitive documents only through the official agency’s approved process. Erica’s website request form is not a Medicaid application.

Once approved, keep an eye on renewal notices.

The state checks whether you still qualify. Keep your mailing address and other contact details current, read notices, and return requested information by the deadline. Your renewal schedule depends on your program and current rules.

If you receive a denial, change, or termination notice, read the reason and appeal instructions. You can ask about a fair hearing; deadlines and whether coverage can continue during an appeal depend on the notice and applicable rules. Learn about eligibility and hearing rights.

What about help at home or in a nursing facility?

Medicaid can help eligible people pay for long-term services, such as nursing-facility care or certain home- and community-based supports. These programs can require both financial eligibility and a review of the help a person needs with daily activities. Home-based programs may have additional requirements or waiting lists.

This is different from Medicare’s limited coverage of skilled nursing care. Ask the state about the specific long-term-care program, what it covers, and how eligibility is assessed. Learn about long-term-care eligibility.

What does “estate recovery” mean?

In certain cases, a state seeks repayment from a person’s estate after death for Medicaid services, including specified long-term-care services received at age 55 or older. Federal protections and hardship procedures apply; state rules differ. This does not mean everyone who uses Medicaid loses their home. Ask your state about its rules before making financial or property decisions. Read the official estate-recovery overview.

What is Medicaid called where I live?

Some states use “Medicaid.” Others use a name such as AHCCCS, MassHealth, or Apple Health. Your card might also show the name of a managed-care plan.

A few common questions

Is Medicaid the same as CHIP?

No. CHIP is the Children’s Health Insurance Program. It can cover children whose families earn too much for Medicaid. States may use one brand for both programs, but the eligibility rules can differ.

Does Medicaid move with me to another state?

Coverage does not simply transfer like a national plan. Contact both states about ending coverage in the old state and applying in the new one. Ask about timing and access to care before your move. Rules for out-of-state care are separate.

What if I receive a bill?

Call the provider and your Medicaid plan or state agency to check whether they used the correct coverage. If you have QMB, tell the provider: you cannot be billed for Medicare-covered deductibles, coinsurance, or copayments. Do not assume every bill is valid or that every service is covered.

Do new Medicaid work rules apply to people with Medicare?

Medicare.gov says the new work or activity requirement for certain adults, beginning January 1, 2027 or earlier in some states, does not apply to people with Medicare. Other eligibility and renewal rules still matter. Read your state’s current notices for details.
