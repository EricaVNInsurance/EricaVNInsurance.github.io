Medicare basics

Original Medicare

Coverage through the federal Medicare program: Part A and Part B. You can add a separate Part D drug plan and may be able to buy Medigap.

See also: Part A — Hospital Insurance · Part B — Medical Insurance · Medigap

Medicare Advantage (MA)

A Medicare-approved private plan that provides your Part A and Part B benefits, usually with Part D. Also called Part C. Plan networks, costs, and rules vary.

See also: Part C · Medicare Advantage with drug coverage (MA-PD) · Original Medicare

Part A — Hospital Insurance

The part of Medicare that helps cover inpatient hospital care, qualifying skilled nursing facility care, hospice, and some home health services.

See also: Inpatient · Benefit period · Part B — Medical Insurance

Part B — Medical Insurance

The part of Medicare that helps cover doctors, outpatient care, medical equipment, and many preventive services.

See also: Outpatient · Preventive services · Part A — Hospital Insurance

Part C

Another name for Medicare Advantage. It is a way to receive Medicare benefits through an approved private health plan.

See also: Medicare Advantage (MA) · Medicare Advantage with drug coverage (MA-PD)

Part D — Prescription drug coverage

Medicare coverage for prescription medicines through private plans. It can be a separate drug plan or part of a Medicare Advantage plan.

See also: Stand-alone Prescription Drug Plan (PDP) · Medicare Advantage with drug coverage (MA-PD) · Formulary

Beneficiary

A person who receives benefits from a program or insurance coverage. A Medicare beneficiary is someone who has Medicare.

See also: Original Medicare · Dual eligible

Medicaid and financial help

Medicaid

A joint federal and state program that helps eligible people with health care costs. Eligibility and benefits vary by state. You can have Medicare and Medicaid together.

See also: Dual eligible · Medicare Savings Program (MSP)

Dual eligible

Someone with Medicare and Medicaid assistance. The help may cover only certain Medicare costs or include full Medicaid benefits.

See also: Medicare Savings Program (MSP) · Medicaid

Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB / QMB-only)

Help with Medicare premiums and covered Part A and Part B cost sharing. Providers cannot bill you for that Medicare cost sharing. QMB-only does not include full Medicaid benefits.

See also: QMB Plus (QMB+) · Medicare Savings Program (MSP)

QMB Plus (QMB+)

QMB assistance together with full Medicaid benefits. You have QMB’s Medicare cost-sharing protections plus the Medicaid benefits available through your state eligibility group.

See also: Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB / QMB-only) · SLMB Plus (SLMB+) · Full-benefit dual eligible (FBDE)

Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB-only)

A Medicare Savings Program that pays your Part B premium. SLMB-only does not include full Medicaid benefits or QMB’s cost-sharing protections.

See also: SLMB Plus (SLMB+) · Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB / QMB-only) · Medicare Savings Program (MSP)

SLMB Plus (SLMB+)

Help with your Part B premium together with full Medicaid benefits. It is a different assistance category from QMB Plus; confirm your coverage with your state.

See also: Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB-only) · QMB Plus (QMB+) · Full-benefit dual eligible (FBDE)

Qualifying Individual (QI)

A Medicare Savings Program that helps pay Part B premiums. You must apply each year. It is for people who do not qualify for other Medicaid coverage.

See also: Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB-only) · Medicare Savings Program (MSP)

Full-benefit dual eligible (FBDE)

A person with Medicare and full Medicaid benefits. Some records use FBDE specifically for full-benefit members outside the QMB and SLMB categories. Confirm your exact state category.

See also: Dual eligible · QMB Plus (QMB+) · SLMB Plus (SLMB+)

Qualified Disabled and Working Individual (QDWI)

A program that helps eligible working people with disabilities pay Part A premiums after they lose premium-free Part A because they returned to work.

See also: Part A — Hospital Insurance · Medicare Savings Program (MSP)

Costs and bills

The examples below use made-up amounts to explain how costs work. They are not Medicare’s current prices or a quote from a plan. Your coverage rules and any financial assistance can change what you pay.

Premium

A monthly payment to keep coverage, even when you do not use care.

Example only — made-up amounts

Imagine a plan premium of $40 a month. You pay $40 even in a month when you do not visit a doctor. Over 12 months, that is $480. Other premiums and costs may also apply.

See also: Deductible · Cost sharing · Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA)

Deductible

An amount you pay before coverage starts paying for services or medicines that have a deductible. Some care may be covered before you meet it.

Example only — made-up amounts

Imagine a $300 deductible. You already paid $200 toward it, so you have $100 left.

Your next covered service has an approved cost of $200:

  1. You pay $100 to finish the deductible.
  2. That leaves $100. If your coinsurance is 20%, you pay another $20.

You pay $120 total. Coverage pays $80.

This assumes the service counts toward that deductible. Meeting a deductible does not always mean your care becomes free.

See also: Coinsurance · Copay · Out-of-pocket limit

Copay

A set dollar amount you pay for a covered service or medicine. Also called a copayment.

Example only

A plan lists a $15 copay for a covered visit. Your copay for that visit is $15. A deductible or other plan rules may also apply.

See also: Coinsurance · Deductible · Cost sharing

Coinsurance

Your share of a covered bill, written as a percentage, such as 20%.

Example only

A covered service has a plan-approved cost of $150. If your share is 20%, you pay $30 after any required deductible. The plan pays the other $120.

See also: Copay · Deductible · Medicare-approved amount

Out-of-pocket costs

Money you pay yourself for care, such as deductibles, copays, and coinsurance.

Example only — made-up amounts

You pay a $20 doctor copay and $10 for a covered prescription. Those two payments add up to $30 out of your pocket. Medical and drug spending may count toward different limits.

See also: Cost sharing · Out-of-pocket limit

Out-of-pocket limit

A yearly cap on your share of certain covered costs. Medicare Advantage has a limit for covered Part A and Part B services. Drug costs follow separate rules. Monthly premiums and care that is not covered generally do not count toward the medical limit.

Example only — made-up amounts

Imagine a Medicare Advantage plan with a $5,000 yearly limit for covered in-network Part A and Part B care. Once the spending that counts reaches $5,000, the plan pays the full cost of that covered in-network care for the rest of the year. Premiums, Part D medicines, and care that is not covered are separate.

See also: Out-of-pocket costs · Cost sharing · Catastrophic coverage (Part D)

Medicare-approved amount

The amount Original Medicare sets for a covered service or item. If your provider accepts this amount as full payment, Medicare pays its share and you pay yours.

Example only — made-up amounts

A provider bills $150, but Original Medicare approves $100 for a covered service. If the provider accepts assignment and your deductible is met, a 20% share is $20—not $30. Medicare pays $80.

See also: Accepting assignment · Excess charge · Coinsurance

Accepting assignment

A provider agrees to accept Medicare’s approved amount as full payment for a covered service. You may still owe a deductible or coinsurance.

Example only — made-up amounts

Using that same $100 approved amount, a provider who accepts assignment cannot add the extra $50 just because the original bill was $150. You may still owe your deductible and coinsurance.

See also: Medicare-approved amount · Excess charge

Cost sharing

Your share of covered care costs, such as a deductible, copay, or coinsurance. Monthly premiums are separate.

Example only — made-up amounts

If your share of a covered doctor visit is a $25 copay, that $25 is cost sharing. Your monthly premium is a separate payment.

See also: Deductible · Copay · Coinsurance

Excess charge

An amount above the Medicare-approved amount that some Original Medicare providers who do not accept assignment may charge. Federal and state rules limit when and how much they can charge.

Example only — made-up amounts

If the Medicare-approved amount is $100 and a provider is allowed to charge $110, the extra $10 is an excess charge. This is separate from any deductible or coinsurance. Whether the charge is allowed depends on the applicable rules.

See also: Accepting assignment · Medicare-approved amount

Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA)

An extra amount added to Part B and/or Part D premiums for some people with higher incomes. Social Security determines whether it applies and sends a notice explaining the decision and appeal rights.

See also: Premium · Late enrollment penalty

Late enrollment penalty

An extra premium you may pay if you sign up late without qualifying for an exception. Part A, Part B, and Part D have different rules. It is usually a recurring charge, not a one-time late fee.

See also: Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) · Creditable drug coverage

Benefit period

How Original Medicare measures certain Part A hospital and skilled nursing facility benefits. It begins when you are admitted to a hospital or skilled nursing facility as an inpatient and ends after 60 days in a row without inpatient hospital care or skilled care in a skilled nursing facility. It is not the same as a calendar year.

Example only — made-up amounts

A hospital stay starts a benefit period. If you return to the hospital after the old period has ended, a new benefit period begins and another Part A deductible may apply—even in the same year.

See also: Part A — Hospital Insurance · Inpatient · Benefit year / Plan year

Allowance

A set amount a plan makes available for certain covered items or services during a stated period. Eligible purchases, providers, and time limits depend on the benefit. It is not automatically cash you can withdraw.

See also: Coverage limits · Rollover / Carryover · Over-the-counter (OTC)

Reimbursement

A payment back to you for an eligible expense you already paid. You may need receipts and a claim. Check the rules first; reimbursement is not a promise that every cost will be repaid.

See also: Claim · Allowance · Explanation of Benefits (EOB)

Rollover / Carryover

Unused benefit amounts moving into a later period, but only if the plan allows it. Some amounts expire monthly, quarterly, or at year-end. Check the exact benefit and deadline.

See also: Allowance · Benefit year / Plan year

Benefit year / Plan year

The period used to organize a plan’s benefits. Medicare Advantage and Part D plan years run January 1 through December 31. This is different from an Original Medicare hospital benefit period.

See also: Benefit period · Annual Notice of Change (ANOC)

Doctors and care

Provider

A person or organization that gives health care, such as a doctor, clinic, or hospital.

See also: Primary care provider (PCP) · Specialist · Network

Primary care provider (PCP)

The health care professional you usually see for routine care, checkups, and many common health concerns.

See also: Provider · Specialist · Referral

Specialist

A doctor who focuses on a particular kind of care, such as heart care or skin conditions.

See also: Primary care provider (PCP) · Referral · Network

Network

The doctors, hospitals, and other health care providers that have agreed to work with a plan.

See also: In-network · Out-of-network · Medicare SELECT

Referral

A written order from your doctor for you to see a specialist or get certain services.

See also: Primary care provider (PCP) · Specialist · Prior authorization

Inpatient

You are formally admitted to a hospital as an inpatient. Staying overnight does not always mean you are an inpatient; ask the hospital about your status.

See also: Outpatient · Observation care · Benefit period

Outpatient

Care you receive without being formally admitted as a hospital inpatient. Hospital observation care is outpatient care, even if you stay overnight.

See also: Inpatient · Observation care

Durable medical equipment (DME)

Reusable medical equipment for use at home, such as a walker, wheelchair, or oxygen equipment. Medicare has rules about which items and suppliers it covers.

See also: Part B — Medical Insurance · Medically necessary

Preventive services

Care that helps prevent illness or find problems early, such as certain vaccines and screenings. Many Medicare-covered preventive services cost nothing when you meet the coverage rules and the provider accepts assignment. Extra services at the same visit may cost you money.

See also: Part B — Medical Insurance · Primary care provider (PCP)

Observation care

Hospital outpatient care used to monitor you and decide what treatment you need. An overnight stay does not automatically make you an inpatient. Ask about your status.

See also: Outpatient · Inpatient · Skilled nursing facility (SNF)

Emergency care

Immediate care for a sudden condition that a reasonable person would think could seriously threaten health without prompt treatment.

See also: Urgently needed care · Out-of-network

Urgently needed care

Care for a sudden illness or injury that needs prompt attention but is not a medical emergency. Coverage and costs depend on the setting and your plan.

See also: Emergency care · Network

Telehealth

Health care provided remotely, such as a video visit. Medicare covers certain services this way; coverage depends on the service and current rules.

See also: Provider · Primary care provider (PCP)

Home health care

Qualifying skilled care provided at home, such as intermittent nursing or therapy. Medicare has eligibility requirements. It does not cover round-the-clock home care or personal care alone.

See also: Skilled nursing facility (SNF) · Custodial care · Caregiver

Hospice care

Comfort-focused care for a terminal illness. Medicare requires certification of a life expectancy of six months or less if the illness follows its usual course. Care can continue with recertification.

See also: Palliative care · Respite care · Caregiver

Palliative care

Care focused on symptom relief and quality of life during serious illness. It can be provided alongside treatment intended to cure an illness. It is not limited to hospice.

See also: Hospice care · Care coordination

Skilled nursing facility (SNF)

A facility providing skilled nursing or rehabilitation care. Medicare may cover qualifying short-term care if its requirements are met; it does not cover an unlimited nursing home stay.

See also: Rehabilitation · Custodial care · Benefit period

Custodial care

Help with daily activities, such as bathing, dressing, or eating. Medicare generally does not cover this when it is the only care you need.

See also: Activities of daily living (ADLs) · Caregiver · Home health care

Rehabilitation

Care that helps you regain or maintain abilities affected by illness, injury, or surgery, such as physical, occupational, or speech therapy. Coverage rules depend on the service and setting.

See also: Physical therapy (PT) · Occupational therapy (OT) · Speech therapy / Speech-language pathology (SLP)

Medically necessary

Care or supplies needed to diagnose or treat a condition and consistent with accepted medical standards. A provider’s recommendation still has to meet the coverage rules.

See also: Prior authorization · Coverage determination (Part D)

Physical therapy (PT)

Therapy to help improve or maintain movement, strength, balance, and physical function. Medicare coverage depends on medical need and other requirements, not simply whether you have reached a set number of visits.

See also: Rehabilitation · Occupational therapy (OT)

Occupational therapy (OT)

Therapy to help you do daily activities, such as dressing, eating, or using your home safely. It can help you learn new ways to manage tasks or maintain function. Coverage requirements apply.

See also: Activities of daily living (ADLs) · Physical therapy (PT)

Speech therapy / Speech-language pathology (SLP)

Therapy for communication, thinking, or swallowing difficulties. It may help improve or maintain function or slow decline. Medicare has medical-need and certification requirements.

See also: Rehabilitation · Medically necessary

Respite care

Temporary care that gives a usual caregiver a break. Medicare can cover certain short inpatient respite stays arranged by a hospice team. It does not mean all caregiver relief or adult day care is covered.

See also: Caregiver · Hospice care

Care coordination

Organizing care across doctors, services, and settings so they work together. It can include sharing care plans and arranging follow-up. This is different from coordination of benefits, which decides which insurance pays first.

See also: Care manager · Coordination of benefits

Care manager

A person on a health plan or provider’s team who helps organize care and connect you with services. Their role and the help available vary. Ask your plan or provider how to reach one.

See also: Care coordination · Caregiver

Activities of daily living (ADLs)

Basic personal tasks such as bathing, dressing, eating, using the toilet, and moving from a bed to a chair. Needing help with these tasks does not by itself mean Medicare will cover ongoing personal care.

See also: Custodial care · Occupational therapy (OT) · Caregiver

Caregiver

Someone who helps a person with health needs or daily activities. A caregiver may be a family member, friend, or paid worker. Being a caregiver does not automatically give permission to act as someone’s legal representative.

See also: Respite care · Care manager · Appointed representative

Medicines

Formulary

A drug plan’s list of covered prescription medicines.

See also: Drug tier · Formulary exception

Drug tier

A level on a plan’s covered drug list that helps determine what you pay. A lower tier often costs less, but each plan sets its own tiers.

See also: Formulary · Part D — Prescription drug coverage

Prior authorization

Approval your plan requires before it will cover certain care or medicines.

See also: Prior authorization renewal (medicines) · Step therapy (ST) · Coverage determination (Part D)

Creditable drug coverage

Prescription coverage expected to pay, on average, at least as much as standard Medicare Part D. This can matter when deciding whether delaying Part D could lead to a penalty.

See also: Part D — Prescription drug coverage · Late enrollment penalty

Generic drug

An FDA-approved version of a brand-name medicine with the same active ingredient, strength, and dosage form. It must work the same way and often costs less. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist about your options.

See also: Brand-name drug · Drug tier

Brand-name drug

A medicine sold under a company’s trademarked name. A generic version may be available. Check your plan’s covered drug list and the price for your exact prescription.

See also: Generic drug · Formulary

Step therapy (ST)

A plan rule that asks you to try a certain medicine first before it covers another medicine for the same condition. Your prescriber can ask for an exception when the first medicine is not appropriate for you.

See also: Prior authorization · Formulary exception

Quantity limit (QL)

A limit on how much medicine a plan covers during a set time. For example, it might cover 30 tablets in a month. Your prescriber can ask for an exception if the limit does not fit your medical needs.

See also: Formulary exception · Coverage determination (Part D)

Preferred pharmacy

An in-network pharmacy where your plan may charge you less for covered prescriptions. A pharmacy can be in the network without being preferred, so check your exact plan and medicines.

See also: Network · Mail-order pharmacy

Formulary exception

A request for a drug plan to cover a medicine not on its covered list or waive certain coverage rules. Your prescriber generally must explain why it is needed.

See also: Formulary · Coverage determination (Part D) · Tiering exception

Coverage determination (Part D)

A drug plan’s decision about whether it will cover a prescription and what you pay. You can request a decision and appeal if you disagree.

See also: Formulary exception · Appeal · Reconsideration

Transition fill

A temporary supply of a medicine when certain new-coverage or formulary situations apply. It gives you time to arrange an exception or alternative; it does not promise ongoing coverage.

See also: Formulary · Formulary exception

Specialty drug

A medicine that may be costly or need special handling, administration, or monitoring. Plans may place these drugs on a specialty tier. Check the exact prescription and pharmacy rules.

See also: Drug tier · Biosimilar

Mail-order pharmacy

A pharmacy that sends prescriptions to your home. Some plans offer longer supplies through mail order. Check the cost, delivery timing, and whether your medicine is eligible.

See also: Preferred pharmacy · Formulary

Biosimilar

An FDA-approved biologic medicine highly similar to an already approved biologic, with no meaningful difference in safety or effectiveness. Ask your prescriber about appropriate options.

See also: Brand-name drug · Specialty drug

Initial coverage stage (Part D)

The drug coverage stage after any applicable deductible. You pay the plan’s copays or coinsurance for covered medicines until the spending that counts reaches the yearly Part D threshold.

See also: Deductible · Catastrophic coverage (Part D)

Catastrophic coverage (Part D)

The stage reached after the yearly Part D out-of-pocket threshold. You pay nothing for covered Part D medicines for the rest of that calendar year. Premiums and noncovered medicines are separate.

See also: Initial coverage stage (Part D) · Out-of-pocket limit

Medicare Prescription Payment Plan

An optional way to spread covered Part D out-of-pocket costs over monthly bills during the year. It changes when you pay, not the total cost of your medicines.

See also: Part D — Prescription drug coverage · Out-of-pocket costs · Extra Help / Low-Income Subsidy (LIS)

Tiering exception

A request for a covered drug to have the lower cost sharing used for a preferred tier. Your prescriber must support why alternatives are not appropriate. Not every drug or tier qualifies, and approval is not automatic.

See also: Drug tier · Formulary exception

Drug interaction

When a medicine’s effects change because of another medicine, food, supplement, alcohol, or a health condition. A pharmacist or prescriber can check for interactions. Do not change your medicines based only on an insurance guide.

See also: Provider · Over-the-counter (OTC)

Prior authorization renewal (medicines)

A new request to continue approval for a medicine when its approved period ends. Whether another request is needed depends on the medicine and plan. Check the approval letter and ask the plan or prescriber before it expires.

See also: Prior authorization · Coverage determination (Part D)

Stand-alone Prescription Drug Plan (PDP)

A separate Medicare Part D plan for prescriptions. It can be used with Original Medicare and certain other coverage types. It does not replace your medical coverage.

See also: Part D — Prescription drug coverage · Original Medicare · Medicare Advantage with drug coverage (MA-PD)

Medicare Advantage with drug coverage (MA-PD)

A Medicare Advantage plan that includes Part D prescription coverage along with Part A and Part B benefits. Your medical and drug benefits come through the same plan.

See also: Medicare Advantage (MA) · Part D — Prescription drug coverage · Stand-alone Prescription Drug Plan (PDP)

Over-the-counter (OTC)

Medicines you can buy without a prescription. Some plans offer an OTC benefit for certain eligible products. A store selling a product does not automatically mean your plan will pay for it.

See also: Allowance · Supplemental benefits · Drug interaction

Coverage and sign-up

Enrollment period

A set time when you can sign up for coverage or make certain changes.

See also: Medicare Advantage (MA) · Medigap

Service area

The place where a health plan is offered. You generally must live there to join.

See also: Enrollment period · Medicare Advantage (MA)

Medigap

Another name for Medicare Supplement Insurance. It helps pay certain costs left to you by Original Medicare.

See also: Original Medicare · Medigap Open Enrollment Period · Guaranteed issue rights (Medigap)

Extra Help / Low-Income Subsidy (LIS)

A Medicare program that helps eligible people with limited income and resources pay Part D drug costs. Receiving it does not by itself mean you have full Medicaid.

See also: Enrollment period · Medicare Advantage (MA)

Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan (D-SNP)

A type of Medicare Advantage plan for people who meet its Medicare and Medicaid requirements. The plan must accept your type of Medicaid assistance.

See also: Dual eligible · Special Needs Plan (SNP) · Medicaid

Health Maintenance Organization (HMO)

A type of Medicare Advantage plan that generally uses a network for your care. Exceptions include emergency care, urgent care, and out-of-area dialysis. You usually choose a primary care doctor and may need referrals. Some HMO-POS plans cover certain out-of-network services.

See also: Network · HMO Point-of-Service (HMO-POS) · Referral

Preferred Provider Organization (PPO)

A type of Medicare Advantage plan with a provider network. You can also get covered care outside the network, usually at a higher cost. You generally do not need specialist referrals. Confirm that the provider agrees to treat you and check your plan’s rules.

See also: Network · Out-of-network

Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)

Your first chance to sign up for Medicare. For most people turning 65, it is a seven-month window: the three months before your birthday month, your birthday month, and the three months after. Timing can differ if you qualify before age 65.

See also: Effective date · Late enrollment penalty

Annual Enrollment Period (AEP)

Also called Medicare Open Enrollment. From October 15 through December 7, you can make certain Medicare Advantage and Part D changes for January 1. This is not the enrollment window for first signing up for Part A or Part B.

See also: Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (MA OEP) · Special Enrollment Period (SEP)

Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (MA OEP)

A January 1–March 31 opportunity for people already in Medicare Advantage to switch Advantage plans or return to Original Medicare and join a separate drug plan. People newly enrolled in Medicare Advantage may also have an opportunity during their first three months with Part A and Part B.

See also: Annual Enrollment Period (AEP) · Disenrollment

Special Enrollment Period (SEP)

A chance to sign up or make certain changes because of a qualifying situation, such as moving or losing other coverage. The changes allowed, deadlines, and start dates depend on the specific situation and type of Medicare coverage.

See also: Enrollment period · Effective date

Effective date

The date your coverage or a coverage change begins. It may be different from the day you submit an application. Check your enrollment confirmation before using new coverage.

Example only — made-up amounts

You submit a request in November, and your confirmation says coverage starts January 1. January 1 is the effective date; submitting the request does not start that coverage immediately.

See also: Enrollment period · Medicare Advantage (MA)

Medicare Savings Program (MSP)

A state-run program that helps eligible people pay certain Medicare premiums. Some programs also help with deductibles, copays, and coinsurance. Eligibility and the kind of help vary; your state decides which program you qualify for.

See also: Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB / QMB-only) · Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB-only) · Qualifying Individual (QI)

Special Needs Plan (SNP)

A Medicare Advantage plan for people with particular qualifying needs, such as certain chronic conditions, institutional care needs, or Medicaid. All SNPs include Part D drug coverage.

See also: Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan (D-SNP) · Chronic Condition Special Needs Plan (C-SNP) · Institutional Special Needs Plan (I-SNP)

Chronic Condition Special Needs Plan (C-SNP)

A Medicare Advantage plan for people with the specific chronic condition or conditions it serves. A diagnosis must meet that plan’s eligibility rules.

See also: Special Needs Plan (SNP) · Medically necessary

Institutional Special Needs Plan (I-SNP)

A Medicare Advantage plan for people who meet its institutional care requirements. Some serve qualifying people living in facilities; others serve people with equivalent care needs at home.

See also: Special Needs Plan (SNP) · Skilled nursing facility (SNF) · Activities of daily living (ADLs)

HMO Point-of-Service (HMO-POS)

An HMO plan that also covers certain services outside its network. Those services may cost more. It does not mean every out-of-network service is covered.

See also: Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) · Out-of-network

Private Fee-for-Service (PFFS) plan

A Medicare Advantage plan that sets payment terms for providers. A provider must agree to the plan’s terms to treat you. Some PFFS plans have networks.

See also: Medicare Advantage (MA) · Accepting assignment

Medicare Medical Savings Account (MSA) plan

A Medicare Advantage option combining high-deductible coverage with a funded medical savings account. It does not include Part D; a separate drug plan may be needed.

See also: Deductible · Stand-alone Prescription Drug Plan (PDP)

Guaranteed issue rights (Medigap)

Protections that let you buy certain Medigap policies in qualifying situations. The insurer cannot reject you or charge more because of your health. The situation, deadline, policy choices, and state rules matter.

See also: Medigap Open Enrollment Period · Trial rights (Medigap)

Medical underwriting

An insurer’s review of your health history when deciding whether to offer a policy and at what price. It may apply to Medigap outside protected enrollment situations. Medicare Advantage does not use Medigap-style medical underwriting.

See also: Pre-existing condition · Guaranteed issue rights (Medigap)

Medigap Open Enrollment Period

A federal six-month window starting the first month you are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. During it, insurers cannot deny a Medigap policy they sell or charge more because of your health. This window does not repeat each fall; state protections may add rights.

See also: Guaranteed issue rights (Medigap) · Medical underwriting · Waiting period

Trial rights (Medigap)

Limited protections in certain first-time Medicare Advantage situations that let you return to Original Medicare and obtain Medigap. Federal trial periods generally last up to 12 months. Check your exact situation, available policies, and application deadlines before changing coverage.

See also: Guaranteed issue rights (Medigap) · Disenrollment · Medigap

Disenrollment

Leaving or being removed from a health or drug plan. The timing and reason affect what happens next. Confirm your replacement coverage and its start date before making a change.

See also: Effective date · Enrollment period · Trial rights (Medigap)

In-network

A doctor, hospital, pharmacy, or other provider that has an agreement with your specific plan. A provider accepting Medicare does not automatically mean it is in your Medicare Advantage network.

See also: Network · Out-of-network · Preferred pharmacy

Out-of-network

A provider without an agreement with your specific plan. Coverage may cost more or may not be available, depending on the plan, service, and situation. Emergency care has different protections.

See also: In-network · Preferred Provider Organization (PPO) · HMO Point-of-Service (HMO-POS)

Supplemental benefits

Extra benefits some Medicare Advantage plans offer beyond Original Medicare’s basic benefits, such as certain dental, vision, or hearing services. Eligibility, providers, costs, and limits vary. These benefits are different from Medigap insurance.

Simple example

A plan may cover a routine eye exam but only help with part of the cost of glasses. An extra benefit does not mean every related service is free.

See also: Allowance · Coverage limits · Over-the-counter (OTC)

Exclusions

Services, items, or circumstances that insurance does not cover. Look at the plan’s documents for the details. A service being excluded is different from one needing approval before coverage.

See also: Coverage limits · Denial

Coverage limits

Rules about how often you can use a benefit or how much the plan will cover for it. These are different from an out-of-pocket limit, which caps certain costs you pay.

Simple example

Imagine an extra dental benefit that covers two routine cleanings a year. A third cleaning may not be covered by that benefit. This is a made-up example; check the actual plan rules.

See also: Allowance · Exclusions · Out-of-pocket limit

Pre-existing condition

A health condition you had before a policy began. It may matter for Medigap underwriting or a permitted waiting period. Your enrollment protections and state rules affect how it can be considered.

See also: Medical underwriting · Waiting period · Guaranteed issue rights (Medigap)

Waiting period

A delay before certain benefits are paid. Some Medigap policies may have a waiting period for costs related to pre-existing conditions. Prior coverage or enrollment protections may reduce or remove it. Check which benefits are affected.

See also: Pre-existing condition · Medigap Open Enrollment Period · Effective date

Guaranteed renewable (Medigap)

A protection that generally lets you keep a Medigap policy despite new health problems when you pay premiums and meet the policy’s requirements. It does not guarantee that your premium will stay the same.

See also: Medigap · Premium · Medical underwriting

Medicare SELECT

A type of Medigap policy that uses a network of hospitals and sometimes doctors for full policy benefits, except in emergencies. It is Medigap, not a Medicare Advantage plan.

See also: Medigap · Network · In-network

Plan documents and decisions

Annual Notice of Change (ANOC)

A notice your plan sends each fall explaining what will change next year, such as coverage and costs. Review it before deciding whether to keep your plan.

See also: Evidence of Coverage (EOC) · Benefit year / Plan year

Evidence of Coverage (EOC)

The detailed document explaining what your plan covers, what you pay, and the rules you must follow to use it.

See also: Summary of Benefits · Exclusions · Coverage limits

Appeal

A request to review a coverage or payment decision you disagree with. Follow the instructions and deadline in the decision notice.

See also: Denial · Reconsideration · Grievance

Claim

A request for Medicare or your plan to pay for care, supplies, or medicine. The provider usually sends it. A claim is the payment request, not the bill you receive.

See also: Reimbursement · Explanation of Benefits (EOB) · Denial

Denial

A decision not to cover or pay for all or part of a requested service, item, or medicine. Read the notice for the reason and how to appeal. A denial does not always mean you have no other options.

See also: Appeal · Coverage determination (Part D)

Grievance

A complaint about care, service, or how you were treated—for example, a customer service problem. Asking a plan to change a coverage or payment decision is usually an appeal.

See also: Appeal · State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)

Explanation of Benefits (EOB)

A statement from your plan showing claims, what it paid, and costs assigned to you. It is not a bill. A Part D EOB summarizes prescription claims and costs; check it for mistakes.

See also: Claim · Medicare Summary Notice (MSN)

Medicare Summary Notice (MSN)

A statement for people with Original Medicare showing services or supplies billed to Medicare, what Medicare paid, and what you may owe. It is not a bill. It also explains how to appeal a decision.

See also: Original Medicare · Claim · Appeal

Summary of Benefits

A shorter overview of a plan’s main coverage and costs. Use it to compare plans, then check the Evidence of Coverage for detailed rules and limits.

See also: Evidence of Coverage (EOC) · Annual Notice of Change (ANOC)

Coordination of benefits

The rules deciding which coverage pays first when you have more than one type of insurance. Having two payers does not always mean every remaining cost is paid.

See also: Primary payer · Secondary payer · Care coordination

Advance Beneficiary Notice of Non-coverage (ABN)

A notice an Original Medicare provider may give before care it expects Medicare will not pay for. Read the cost estimate, reason, choices, and appeal information before deciding.

See also: Exclusions · Medicare-approved amount · Appeal

Primary payer

The insurance or program that pays first for a claim, up to its coverage limits. Which payer comes first depends on your situation.

See also: Secondary payer · Coordination of benefits

Secondary payer

Coverage that considers a claim after the primary payer. It pays according to its own rules and may not pay the whole balance.

See also: Primary payer · Coordination of benefits

Appointed representative

Someone you formally choose to help with a Medicare claim, appeal, grievance, or request. The appointment gives specific authority; it does not automatically grant control over all your affairs.

See also: Caregiver · Appeal

State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)

A program offering free, personalized Medicare counseling for you and your family. SHIP counselors are not connected to an insurance company or health plan. They can help with coverage, rights, and billing questions.

See also: Medicare Savings Program (MSP) · Appeal · Medicare fraud

Medicare fraud

Intentionally deceiving Medicare to obtain improper payment or benefits, such as billing for services never provided. An unfamiliar charge can also be an error; review it with the provider or report suspected fraud.

See also: Identity theft · Claim

Identity theft

Using someone’s personal information without permission. Medical identity theft can involve a stolen Medicare number used for care or false claims. Protect your information and report suspected misuse.

See also: Medicare fraud · Medicare Summary Notice (MSN)

Reconsideration

A review at a particular step of a Medicare appeal. Who reviews it and when it happens differ between Original Medicare, Medicare Advantage, and Part D. Follow the instructions and deadline on your decision notice.

See also: Appeal · Coverage determination (Part D)
