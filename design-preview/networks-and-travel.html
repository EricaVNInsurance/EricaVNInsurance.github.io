Comparing options

Doctors, networks, and care in other states.

Compare HMO, HMO-POS, PPO, and Original Medicare with Medigap before you book care or travel.

What is a provider network?

A network is the group of doctors, hospitals, and other providers that have agreements with your specific plan. In-network means the provider is part of that network. Out-of-network means the provider does not have that agreement.

“We accept Medicare” is not the same as “We are in your Medicare Advantage plan’s network.” Check the full plan name, plan ID, year, provider, and office location. Two plans from the same company can have different networks.

Referral

Your doctor directs you to a specialist or service.

Prior authorization

The plan reviews certain care before it is provided. A referral and an authorization are different things.

HMO: a network-centered approach.

Health Maintenance Organization. Routine medical care generally needs to come from network providers. You usually select a primary care doctor, and most HMOs require referrals for specialists. Certain services may need prior approval.

Another state: Do not assume a routine visit is covered because the doctor accepts Medicare. The doctor may be outside your plan’s network. Unless an exception or plan arrangement applies, you could owe the full bill.

Exceptions include emergency care, out-of-area urgently needed care, and temporary out-of-area dialysis. These exceptions do not make every planned appointment away from home covered.

HMO-POS: an HMO with specified out-of-network benefits.

HMO Point-of-Service. It follows HMO rules but can cover certain services outside its network, often at a higher cost.

Another state: Ask which exact services have the point-of-service benefit, whether the provider can be used, and what limits or approvals apply. “POS” does not mean every out-of-network doctor visit or procedure is covered. It is not automatically the same flexibility as a PPO.

Example: Elena checks what POS actually covers.

Elena sees “HMO-POS” on her card and wants a routine specialist visit in another state. Before booking, she asks whether that specific medical service is included in the POS benefit. The label alone does not answer the question.

PPO: a network, with out-of-network coverage.

Preferred Provider Organization. You can generally use network or out-of-network providers for covered services. In-network care usually costs less. PPOs do not require a primary care doctor or specialist referrals.

Another state: A covered visit can generally use an eligible out-of-network provider. The provider must agree to treat you; a PPO card does not guarantee an appointment with every doctor. Confirm coverage, the provider’s Medicare status, and your share of the bill before planned care.

Do PPOs require prior authorization?

Some in-network services may require it. CMS says Medicare Advantage PPOs cannot require prior authorization for covered out-of-network services. The service must still meet coverage and medical-necessity requirements. You can ask the plan for a coverage decision before care; that check is different from a required authorization.

Check the plan’s in-network and combined in-/out-of-network yearly medical spending limits. Lower copays at home do not mean the same costs apply away from home.

Read Medicare.gov’s PPO rules · CMS: Prior authorization exceptions (question 11)

Original Medicare + Medigap: a different setup.

With Original Medicare, you can generally use doctors and hospitals that take Medicare anywhere in the U.S. A standard Medigap policy helps pay your share of Medicare-covered costs according to its benefits. Medigap is supplemental insurance, not a Medicare Advantage provider network.

Another state: An eligible Medicare-covered visit is generally handled the same way: Medicare pays first, then Medigap helps with the remaining covered costs. Crossing a state line does not, by itself, turn a visit into out-of-network care under standard Medigap. Confirm the provider accepts Medicare and is willing to see you.

You may still owe costs your policy does not pay. Original Medicare’s coverage rules apply; services Medicare does not cover are not automatically covered by Medigap. You generally do not need a specialist referral under Original Medicare.

The exception to know: Medicare SELECT.

Medicare SELECT is a type of Medigap policy with network restrictions for full policy benefits—usually hospitals and sometimes doctors, except in emergencies. Outside that network, Original Medicare can still pay its share, but SELECT may not pay all the supplemental benefits you expected. Check whether your policy says “SELECT.”

Example: Rosa sees doctors in Michigan and Florida.

Rosa spends winters with family in Florida and wants a scheduled heart-specialist checkup there. It is routine follow-up, not an emergency. Here is what she would check with each approach:

  • HMO: Is the Florida specialist in her exact plan’s network, or does her plan arrange coverage for this visit?
  • HMO-POS: Does her POS benefit include this particular specialist service? What would she owe?
  • PPO: Is the specialist in-network? If not, will the specialist see her as an out-of-network member, and what cost sharing applies?
  • Original Medicare + standard Medigap: Does the specialist take Medicare and accept her as a patient? What does her particular Medigap policy pay toward the covered visit?

Rosa compares access and total costs instead of assuming “PPO” and “Medigap” work the same way. This fictional example is a checklist, not a promise that a particular visit will be covered.

Emergency care is different from a scheduled visit.

Medicare Advantage plans cover emergency care and qualifying urgently needed care when you are away from home. Being at an urgent-care clinic does not automatically make a routine visit “urgently needed.” The circumstances and need for immediate care matter.

Example: Daniel gets sick during a trip.

Daniel develops an unexpected illness while visiting Oregon and needs prompt care. That is a different coverage question from scheduling a routine checkup there. Once the immediate problem is treated, later planned follow-up may need to follow his plan’s usual network rules.

In an emergency, get care right away. For non-emergency follow-up, contact the plan about where to receive care and what it covers. Temporary out-of-area dialysis also has special protection; coordinate arrangements before traveling.

What about other plan names?

D-SNP, C-SNP, and I-SNP

Special Needs Plans describe who a plan serves: people with Medicare and Medicaid, certain qualifying chronic conditions, or specified institutional care needs. SNPs use HMO or PPO structures. A D-SNP label does not, by itself, tell you whether routine out-of-network care is covered.

Read about Medicare, Medicaid, and D-SNPs.

PFFS — Private Fee-for-Service

This is a Medicare Advantage plan type, not Original Medicare or Medigap. A Medicare-approved provider must agree to the plan’s payment terms and to treat you. Some PFFS plans have networks. Outside the network, acceptance may need to be confirmed for each visit; costs may differ. It is not a blanket “go anywhere” promise.

MSA — Medical Savings Account

This Medicare Advantage arrangement combines a high-deductible plan and a savings account. MSAs usually have no provider network, but the provider must be eligible and agree to treat you. You can have substantial costs before meeting the deductible. It does not work like Medigap. Availability and enrollment restrictions apply.

Medicare Cost Plans

Available in limited areas, these are different from Medicare Advantage. You can use the plan’s network, or use providers who take Medicare and pay Original Medicare costs for that care. Ask how the Cost Plan and Original Medicare handle a particular visit.

Local PPO versus regional PPO

These labels describe how the Medicare Advantage plan’s service area is organized. Neither label guarantees that every provider in another state is in-network. Check the exact plan directory and out-of-network rules.

Before booking care away from home.

  1. Check the exact provider. Confirm the doctor, facility, and office location with both the plan and provider. A hospital being in-network does not answer every question about the doctors practicing there.
  2. Name the service. Ask about the actual visit, test, procedure, or treatment—not just “travel coverage.”
  3. Check the payment rules. Review network status, referrals, any applicable authorization requirements, and medical-necessity rules.
  4. Ask what you may owe. Confirm the copay, coinsurance, deductible, and which medical out-of-pocket limit applies.
  5. Check prescriptions separately. A medical network and a Part D pharmacy network are different. Ask about a network pharmacy at your destination and refill timing.

The Evidence of Coverage explains the plan’s rules. Keep confirmation details for planned care. If needed covered care is unavailable in the network, ask the plan to arrange access rather than assuming you must pay privately.

Long trips and moving are different.

Medicare Advantage and Part D plans have service areas. Tell the plan before a long stay or permanent move: how long you can remain enrolled and how travel benefits work depend on the rules and plan arrangements. Moving outside a service area may give you a Special Enrollment Period.

With Medigap, check the policy and insurer before a move; premiums and available policies can vary. Do not cancel current coverage before confirming any replacement and your right to buy it.

Travel outside the U.S. has different rules.

Original Medicare usually does not cover foreign care, with limited exceptions. Some Medigap policies cover limited foreign-travel emergency care. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer extra foreign emergency or urgent-care benefits. None of these labels guarantees routine worldwide coverage. Check limits, exclusions, and whether separate travel medical insurance is needed.
